About the Workshop

Context and Purpose

Digital evidence now sits at the centre of how we investigate crime, respond to cyber incidents, and settle disputes over what was actually said, shown, or shared. Messaging apps, social platforms, and synthetic media have multiplied the volume of that evidence while making its origin and integrity harder to establish. Forensic readiness, the capacity to collect, preserve, and rely on digital evidence before an incident rather than scrambling after one, has become a precondition for trust in social communications.

This one-day workshop, hosted by the UQ Cyber Research Centre at The University of Queensland, brings investigators, practitioners, media researchers, clinicians, and cybersecurity specialists together. The programme is organised into three connected themes: digital investigations and forensic readiness; misinformation, verification, and trustworthy communications; and cyber resilience, compliance, and threat detection.

Each themed block brings related talks, hands-on learning, and student project presentations into one conversation. Prof. Parag Rughani's practical session connects directly with Jack Holman and Noah Trevena's joint malware-analysis project. Dr Anne Kruger's work on misinformation and verification complements UQ's technical research on manipulated-media authentication and provenance. The closing block connects healthcare evidence assurance with malware and network-traffic analysis.

The workshop also showcases AEA Ignite research on the Authentication and Provenance of Manipulated Media (APMM), including complementary work by Gagandeep Singh, Samudi Amarasinghe, and Lukas Zhang.

Workshop Focus

Focus Areas

Three questions connect the talks, projects, and demonstrations across the day.

Focus 1Readiness by Design

Planning evidence collection, logging, retention, and chain of custody before an incident occurs, so that organisations can answer what happened quickly and defensibly instead of reconstructing it from whatever survived.

Focus 2Trust in Social Communications

Establishing the provenance and authenticity of messages, images, voice, and video shared across social platforms, including detection of manipulated and synthetic media, provenance verification, and the evidential weight such content can carry. This is the core question behind our AEA Ignite APMM research.

Focus 3Resilience and Response

Forensic investigation that supports recovery rather than delaying it, with particular attention to healthcare settings where systems must stay available, patient data must stay protected, and findings must feed back into stronger defences.

09:15 · Opening keynote
Mike Cohen · Rapid7

Forensic Readiness through endpoint agents

Digital forensics has become a standard part of the incident response process. Most DFIR teams now routinely deploy agent-based triage software to collect forensic evidence.

Ultimately, the purpose of DFIR is to answer critical questions about the incident — such as what was the initial compromise vector, what information was accessed, and how long was the dwell time before the adversaries were evicted.

Forensic-capable agents, such as Velociraptor, answer these questions by analysing digital forensic information sources that are found on the endpoint.

However, most digital forensic artefacts are incidental to the incident itself. These sources were typically never designed to provide the type of information needed in a DFIR investigation.

This talk will discuss the different options for actively assisting the DFIR process by proactively increasing forensic readiness. This approach ranges from simply enhancing logging and auditing in the standard OS build to actively monitoring events on the endpoint. As an example, we show the different approaches with the Velociraptor DFIR agent.

15:10 · Threat detection
Prof. Rajat Subhra Chakraborty · Indian Institute of Technology Kharagpur

See it to Sieve It: Visual Techniques in Malware Binary and Network Traffic Detection and Classification

Classification of malware binaries and network traffic are being performed increasingly by advanced machine learning techniques. However, the wide variety of malware and encrypted network traffic have proven challenging to these techniques. In this talk, I will share some of my research group's recent work on applying visual techniques for these tasks. For malware binaries, the repeated usage patterns of particular instructions create distinctive visual patterns. Similarly, the temporal arrival patterns of different classes of network traffic, when plotted, create graphs with distinctive visual features. These visual cues make it possible for a well-trained machine learning classifier to detect and classify malware binary or network traffic with high accuracy.

The talk will be accompanied by a live online demo by a graduate student researcher (Anurag).

14:40 · Cyber resilience
Matt Walker · CyberAgile

Trust, Provenance, and Compliance: A Tamper-Evident Architecture for Small Healthcare Providers

For small healthcare practices, protecting patient information is only part of the challenge. Providers must also be able to demonstrate what security measures were in place, how incidents were assessed, and when key decisions and notifications occurred. Producing reliable, dated, and retained evidence can be particularly difficult for practices with only four to ten staff. At the same time, automated systems and AI-assisted workflows create new risks: even an audit trail can be altered or misrepresented.

This talk presents a practical, three-layer architecture for building trustworthy evidence into day-to-day healthcare operations. A standardised managed environment provides consistent security controls across practices; an automated evidence pipeline produces compliance artefacts as a by-product of normal operations; and a tamper-evident ledger records proof of those artefacts without storing patient health information. The presentation maps compliance obligations to technical controls, outlines the threats the ledger does and does not address, and considers the economics of sharing the approach across a network of approximately twenty or more practices.
